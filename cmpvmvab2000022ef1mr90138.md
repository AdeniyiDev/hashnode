It's 2:17 AM.  
Your phone starts vibrating aggressively on the bedside table.

**CRITICAL ALERT — Production API Error Rate Above Threshold**

You immediately grab your phone.  
Customers are reporting issues.  
The dashboard is full of red graphs.  
Slack notifications are flooding in.  
Management is asking for updates.  
And suddenly, despite years of experience, your brain feels slower than usual.  
You know how to troubleshoot systems.  
You know Kubernetes.  
You know Linux.  
You know cloud infrastructure.  
So why does everything become harder during an incident?  
The answer has less to do with technical skills and more to do with psychology.

### The Brain Doesn't Know It's "Just a Server"

When an incident begins, your brain doesn't distinguish between a production outage and a physical threat.  
To your nervous system, both situations trigger a stress response.  
Heart rate increases.  
Breathing becomes faster.  
Adrenaline floods the body.  
Your brain shifts into survival mode.  
This response evolved to help humans escape danger.  
The problem is that debugging a failing service requires clear thinking, patience, and logical reasoning—not panic.  
Ironically, the moment you need your brain the most is often the moment stress makes it perform worse.

### Tunnel Vision Takes Over

One of the most common mistakes during incidents is becoming obsessed with the first theory that comes to mind.  
Maybe CPU usage is high.  
Maybe a deployment happened recently.  
Maybe the database looks suspicious.  
Once the brain forms an explanation, it starts searching for evidence that supports it.  
This is called confirmation bias.  
Instead of investigating broadly, engineers often focus on proving their theory correct.  
The result?  
Valuable time gets wasted while the real cause remains hidden.

### The Pressure of Being Watched

Incidents rarely happen in isolation.  
While you're troubleshooting, other people are waiting for answers.  
Customers want updates.  
Managers want timelines.  
Teammates want direction.  
Every message creates additional pressure.  
The challenge is that uncertainty is normal during incidents.  
You often don't know the root cause immediately.  
Yet many engineers feel compelled to provide instant answers.  
This pressure can lead to rushed decisions, risky fixes, and unnecessary changes that make the situation worse.

### Why Experienced Engineers Still Panic

Many people assume panic disappears with experience.  
It doesn't.  
Even senior engineers feel stress during critical outages.  
The difference is that experienced engineers have learned to trust processes instead of emotions.  
Rather than reacting impulsively, they rely on runbooks.  
They gather evidence.  
They verify assumptions.  
They follow systematic troubleshooting steps.  
Experience doesn't remove stress.  
It reduces the chance that stress controls your actions.

### The Most Dangerous Thought During an Incident

There's one thought that causes more problems than almost anything else:  
"I think I know what's wrong."  
The moment you stop investigating and start assuming, mistakes become more likely.  
Good incident responders stay curious.  
They collect data before drawing conclusions.  
They ask questions such as:

*   What changed recently?
    
*   What systems are affected?
    
*   What evidence supports this theory?
    
*   What evidence contradicts it?
    

Facts first. Assumptions later.

### How to Stay Calm During Production Incidents

The goal isn't to eliminate stress.  
The goal is to prevent stress from controlling your decisions.  
A few habits help tremendously:

1.  Follow a Checklist
    

Pilots use checklists during emergencies for a reason.  
Stress reduces memory and decision-making quality.  
A checklist keeps you focused on the next step.

2\. Gather Evidence Before Acting

Avoid making changes based on guesses.  
Logs, metrics, events, and traces should guide your decisions.

3\. Communicate Clearly

If you don't know the root cause yet, say so.  
Honest updates are better than confident guesses.

4\. Slow Down to Speed Up

Many incidents become worse because someone rushed a fix.  
A careful five-minute investigation can save hours of recovery time.

### Final Thoughts

Production incidents are rarely just technical events.  
They're psychological events too.  
The dashboards, alerts, and failing services are only half the battle.  
The other half is managing stress, avoiding assumptions, and staying disciplined when pressure is highest.  
The engineers who handle incidents best aren't always the smartest people in the room.  
They're often the ones who remain calm, trust the process, and make decisions based on evidence rather than panic.  
Because during an outage, your biggest enemy isn't always the broken system.  
Sometimes it's your own brain.
